# How Pilots Land When They Cannot See the Runway

Cloud, fog, rain, or darkness can hide a runway until very late in an approach. That does not leave pilots feeling their way through the sky.

They follow a published **instrument approach** that provides a precise path and a clear point at which to land or climb away.

## 📡 1. Instruments Lead Towards the Runway

A common system is the **ILS**, or Instrument Landing System. Radio signals near the runway provide lateral guidance toward the centerline and vertical guidance down a safe descent path.

Many airports also have approaches based on satellite navigation or other ground equipment. Cockpit displays show whether the aircraft is left, right, high, or low relative to the planned path.

Air traffic control helps sequence the flight, but the approach guidance and published procedure give the crew the detailed route to follow.

## 🧑‍✈️ 2. The Crew Builds a Stable Approach

Before descending, pilots review the procedure, weather, runway, missed-approach route, and the lowest permitted point. They configure the aircraft in stages by reducing speed, extending flaps, and lowering the landing gear.

The autopilot may follow the guidance, or a pilot may fly it manually. Either way, both pilots monitor the flight path, speed, systems, and each other. Automation changes who moves the controls; it does not remove the crew from the decision.

## 👀 3. There Is a Firm Decision Point

Most instrument approaches have a **minimum**, such as a decision altitude, decision height, or minimum descent altitude. By that point, the crew must normally have the required visual references and be able to continue safely.

Those references may include approach lights, runway-edge lights, or markings rather than the entire runway at once.

If the required view is not available, the pilots perform a **go-around**: they add power, climb, and follow a prepared route. They may try again, wait for improvement, or divert. A go-around is a normal safety decision, not a failed landing.

## 🤖 4. Autoland Is Available Only in the Right Conditions

Some aircraft can use **autoland**, in which automatic systems control the final approach, touchdown, and part of the rollout. It is used only when the aircraft, runway equipment, operator, and crew are all approved for it.

Pilots set up and monitor the system throughout. Airports may also protect the sensitive ILS signal area from vehicles and other aircraft during low-visibility operations.

Under certain tightly controlled Category III procedures, an approved autoland may continue to touchdown without the usual outside view. That is the exception, not a general ability to land anywhere in “zero visibility.” Not every poor-weather landing is automatic, and not every runway supports an autoland.

## 💡 Simple Way to Think About It

An instrument approach is like:
> following an invisible, carefully measured ramp toward the runway — with a marked exit point if the view is not good enough to finish.

## 🟢 Quick Fact

In fog, the first useful sight may be a row of approach lights rather than the runway surface itself.

Pilots do not need to see the runway from miles away. They need accurate guidance, a stable aircraft, and either the required visual reference by a precisely defined point or a fully approved low-visibility autoland operation — otherwise, they safely climb away.
